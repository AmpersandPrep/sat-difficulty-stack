# Coverage and heuristics, dictated by Lydia (begun Nov 2, 2026)

Lydia's own classroom knowledge, skill by skill, in her words. These are pre-assumptions about the student population: what class emphasized, what students reach for first, and what they rarely think of on their own. A skill's entries come with every question in that skill. They are background, not diagnostics, and their presence is not a signal about any particular question.

## Linear equations in two variables

Same as Systems, below (Lydia, Nov 2, 2026: the heuristics are the same; College Board splits these into separate skills, students don't experience them separately).

## Systems of two linear equations in two variables

- Students often don't recognize parallel lines, perpendicular lines. Basically they silo geometry and linear algebra separate.
- They don't always understand ax + by = c.
- Parametrized form is unfamiliar.
- On "how many solutions" stems, students reach for the discriminant: "I tried to find the discriminate but I couldn't figure that out" (student quote, via Lydia, Nov 2, 2026).
- The bait is double (Lydia, Nov 2, 2026, on this question): "Not only are they baited to think of the discriminant, they are also baited not to think about parallel lines." The stem language pulls toward the wrong tool while the presentation keeps the parallel relationship out of view.
- Fourth element of this question (Lydia, Nov 2, 2026): it looks deceptively easy. (Her full count for 797a81fb: novel type, an unsolvable system as the prime example of a Hard question, the same-line pull toward infinitely many, and the deceptively easy look.)

## Linear functions

- It's usually function notation that gets tricky.
- If something is written other than f(x) = y, it gets tricky. [One word in Lydia's dictation came through garbled ("soorning"); read as "written," to be corrected if wrong.]
- Operations with functions get tricky.

## Linear equations in one variable

- "# of solutions" makes them think of quadratics.

## Linear inequalities in one or two variables

- They can mix up greater or less than easily.

## Equivalent expressions

- They actually don't usually know the difference between equivalent and an equation.
- They might try to solve it.

## Nonlinear equations in one variable and systems of equations in two variables

- They don't always recognize how to find one solution with the discriminant.

## Nonlinear functions

- They quite often don't understand the relationship between quadratics and quadrilaterals. One of the first things Lydia asks a student is why we say "square" when we take something to the second power; most of them don't know why. They literally don't connect quadrilaterals with quadratics.
- Example: a rectangle with one side x and the other side x + 3, area 32. Students say "I don't know how to solve this," even though it's a really easy factoring problem. And they don't know how to handle it because they worry x will have a negative value; they don't realize that distance is always positive, so the negative root is the one you throw out.
- With exponents they don't always realize if the constant term is 0 the y int is 1 not 0.

## Nonlinear functions (continued, Nov 2 voice call)

- Parabola symmetry: students don't always realize the h value, the line of symmetry, is the average of two x-values that have the same y. They have a hard time with the symmetry of a parabola.
- The first term of the quadratic formula is that same average, and it means nothing to them: the quadratic formula is just runes on the paper, even though they probably know how to use it.
- Sum of solutions and product of solutions to a quadratic: most students have to be taught these. Lydia doesn't think she's had anyone who knew them already. Not because they're not smart; nobody teaches it until SAT prep, where it's taught all the time. The only kids who know it are ones who were already practicing the SAT.
- Polynomials and place value: students don't always understand the relationship between a polynomial and place value. It doesn't come up that much, but polynomial long division and the remainder rule: "I have no idea what's going on with the remainder rule."
- Rational expressions: numerator x + 3, denominator x squared + 6x + 9. Students don't know what to do. They don't always connect division with factoring; factoring is just a process to them, they don't understand that it's division or multiplication (so the bottom being (x + 3) squared doesn't register as something that can cancel).

## One-variable data: Distributions and measures of center and spread

- Averages backwards: when they're given the average and have to find something else (Jimmy got an 80, 82, 83, forgot the last test, find it from the overall average), that's confusing, because they're used to finding the average. "What am I doing?" (Lydia's related tip is deliberately NOT recorded here, at her direction.)
- Mode not in the middle: 2, 2, 2, 2, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11. The mode sitting at the start confuses them; they expect it in the middle.
- Lydia herself was caught by a median/mode edge case once, on the paper SAT before it went digital; she doesn't remember the exact question and doubts it can be found. Noted so the category isn't treated as always tidy.
- Histograms and ranges: if a bin is 0 through 9 and they're asked the least possible distance between that bin and the next bin, a lot of students say 5 or 10. They won't say 1. They don't think top of the range versus bottom of the range; they think middle.
- Standard deviation: NO entry, by Lydia's call. Students usually don't know anything about it; once they're taught it, they're usually not confused by it. Teaching it doesn't leave confusable heuristics behind. Histograms generally: even students who don't know stats can usually work one out.

## Percentages

- Backing into the original amount: if the sweater was on sale for 20% and the final price was $5, what was the original price? They have a hard time when they have to back into the thing they're taking a percentage of, instead of finding the percentage of that thing. Same backward shape as the averages.

## Two-variable data: Models and scatterplots

- Axis scales that aren't one-to-one: the x-axis broken into 15-minute increments (really 0.25), the y-axis number of boats rented. Students like to think the little grid is always one-to-one. And the distractor will almost always include the slope you'd get if you made exactly that mistake; the distractor assumes the misread.

## Inference from sample statistics and margin of error

- Must versus could: on margin of error questions, students get real weird with "must" and "could." The test loves "it must be" where the thing is really improbable but slightly possible.

## Geometry and Trigonometry (Lydia's grades included where she gave them)

- A diagram described in words instead of given as a diagram is always going to be more difficult.
- Diagram not drawn to scale: automatic Medium. Throw anything else in there and it's going to be Hard.
- Isosceles triangle, one data point given: students think they're missing something, because they have to work out that an isosceles gives them two sides from one. Definitely Medium (or Easy-type distractor), not Hard.
- Tangent equals negative one for 45 degrees: same idea as the isosceles, because the isosceles right triangle is a one-to-one ratio. Medium.
- Inverse trig: given the ratio and asked for the angle (inverse sine). Lydia has never seen an easy inverse sine question; always Medium or Hard. And an inverse function gets confused with the reciprocal: see sin with a negative-one exponent and they think cosecant. The notation itself confuses them.
- Pythagorean triplets in decimal form: 0.6, 0.8, and 1. Students won't recognize that as an iteration of the 3-4-5 triangle, because it's not integers, and the 1 hides that it's 10 (scaled by ten). This one got Lydia too, briefly.
- Doubling versus squaring, the 2-4-8-16 family: two plus two is four, two times two is four, two squared is four. With doubling or halving in anything, radians in particular, they mix up squares and doubles. Radius of a circle is 4: there will almost always be an answer that equals 8, because instead of squaring 4 to get 16 (in the circle equation) they double it. They love 2, 4, 8, and 16, and test makers love confusing kids with them. Seeing 2, 4, 8, or 16 gives students a sense of calm, "those are numbers I understand," so the problem is deceptively hard: kids answer 8 all day when they know it's 16; it's not that they don't know, they went too fast. When those numbers appear, look at the distractors for the doubling/halving weirdness. Same idea: asked for the radius, the diameter will be there; asked for the diameter, the radius will be there.
- Shape switching: if they have to do triangle work or rectangle or square work but they're looking at a circle, that'll be hard, or vice versa.
- Area of a triangle: students have memorized one-half base times height without understanding. They will forget so fast that one-half times something is the same as divided by two. They know it; they forget it in the moment.
- Cones and pyramids: students' minds flip. They don't understand that a cone or pyramid is just a third of the prism. That confuses them a lot.
- Mensuration: when measuring off the diagram is obviously what you should do, their geometry teachers have told them not to do that, to use proofs, so they say "well, I don't know how to do that" and insist they need a formula. "But that's obviously acute. Don't pick 180." Mensuration is the way to the answer and they have a hard time with that.
- Sine and cosine relationship: given a right triangle, cosine is 0.8, what is the sine of 90 minus theta? They don't realize that's also just 0.8. "I don't know the formula. How do I do it?"

## General (cross-skill, "sneaks into all problems")

- If it is asking for # of solutions, students tend to think about the discriminant. (Moved here by Lydia, Nov 2, 2026, after the v2 two-question run: it began on the Systems card, but the reach happens wherever solution-count language appears.)
- Inverted negatives are absolutely tricky (Lydia, Nov 2, 2026): two values that match except for the sign, like 36 and −36. Students register the match and blow past the sign.

- Integers: the word itself confuses them. If a problem says there's an integer between 0 and 2, it should be a really easy process of elimination, because there's only one. Instead they think of any decimal in between, a range, infinitely many, and the problem feels huge.
- Solving for constants: they're so used to solving for x and y that being given x and y (or a point) and asked to solve for a constant gets really difficult, even though they were taught to do it. Especially in a word problem.
- Fractional, negative, or zero exponents: probably at least a Medium, anywhere they appear. Medium students have often never considered a fractional exponent; a cube or fourth root, they have no idea what's going on. Something to the three-over-two power cannot be understood as repeated multiplication, so it doesn't make sense to them; it looks like runes on the paper. (The strongest students don't have a problem with it.) Shows up in polynomials, in trigonometry via Pythagoras, everywhere.
- Square root of x squared: "I don't know what that is." Students get confused by it; Lydia remembers being confused by it herself. Related: rationalizing a denominator, multiply root 3 by root 3, and asked what the denominator is they say "square root of nine" instead of 3.
- The clever shortcut in place of calculus: if something clever could solve something that would normally take calculus, that's difficult. Example: the solution requires understanding the difference of squares is just factors, the binomial conjugates; written so that if it weren't exactly the difference of squares, you would need calculus. Students stall on "wait, how do you do this?" until the one particular example they know clicks.
- Umbrella (Lydia, Nov 2): almost all of these are about something counterintuitive. Not "math is hard."

## Central Ideas and Details

- Answer choices that use the same buzzwords as the passage and are right except for one thing switched. Students read for the echo and pick the familiar wording.

## Command of Evidence

- Non-straightforward graphs: the x value isn't time, or time appears in a different way; the key does the work (light gray bar is the 1990s, dark gray bar is the 2000s) while the x-axis is North America, South America. Students are so used to time being on the x-axis that this gets hard.
- The flashy value is not the answer: questions asking for the second place, the penultimate conclusion. Lizards running away: some run at 100% speed, but running at 100% speed is not ideal in all situations; students see the 100% on the graph and stop there. Frogs that eat their tadpoles: the frog that ate the most had a high amount of the chemical, but the second-highest frog didn't have that chemical at all, so the assertion can't be made. The thing you need from the graph is not the flashy thing.
- A graph whose upward slope looks like speeding up but is actually the percentage of species who escape at that speed. Lydia's appraiser reaction: she would never present data this way; a non-appraiser has to be able to understand it. Her framing to students: it's a critical thinking test, and misleading statistics are the point.

## Cross-Text Connections

- Point-of-view switch: saying what both authors would agree on, or what they'd say to new data, is comparatively easy to rule out. Having to respond AS one author TO the other (what would the author of passage two say to the author of passage one) is significantly harder. Lydia calls it empathy transference. Anytime there's a point-of-view switch, that's almost always an increase in difficulty.

## Text Structure and Purpose

- Wit: Lydia's definition for students: a wink, a nod, or a joke between the author and the reader that the characters don't hold. Students usually don't register that wit is happening at all in a timed passage; if reminded (you read Jane Austen, you know what wit is) they know it fine. The difficulty is recognition in the moment, not comprehension.
- Animacy and metaphor: if there is a level of animacy in the answer choices or in metaphor, students get confused, because they think "we're not supposed to do personification," so an author using animacy as a witty aside reads to them as wrong. It's not just play; it can be metaphor generally.

## Craft and Structure

- A correct answer to a different question: they ask what a sentence or word is doing here, and one of the answers describes the main purpose, which isn't the question. Students love that answer because it's true of the passage and answers something, just not what was asked.

## Words in Context

- A "no" or "not" somewhere in the sentence: the definition feels like the opposite of what it ought to be, and their brain grabs the word they know.
- A common meaning and a less common meaning, especially on a relatively common word: "exploit." Students tend to think it's negative (exploitation, being persecuted), but "the scientists exploit the properties of the sample" is neutral. A niche use of a word makes it hard. (Difficult words being difficult is not itself the heuristic.)

## Transitions

- However and despite: hard (Lydia's placement, correcting an initial misfiling under Rhetorical Synthesis).

## Rhetorical Synthesis

- Students have memorized a rule: they don't have to read the notes, they just answer the question. That works most of the time, which is scary, because when they actually have to fact-check the notes and synthesize two things, it gets hard.

## Boundaries

- Two independent clauses with a nonessential phrase or a dependent clause between them: students will oftentimes not realize the two independent clauses still need an independent clause boundary separation. They think independent clause, dependent clause, independent clause is fine. It's not. (Same seam logic as Lydia's couldbe principle.)

## Form, Structure, and Sense

- Agreement questions are logic problems, not conjugation tests: almost every agreement question has some sort of trick. The test doesn't really test irregular verbs; students all know how to conjugate. The problem is finding the true subject.
- Unambiguous-amount subjects: oceans, peoples, species. Is that singular or plural? That can really mess with a kid's head.
- "One of many": singular (Lydia corrected herself mid-dictation: "I actually think it's the opposite. I think that's singular."). Students hear "many" and reach for plural.
- A nonessential aside immediately after the subject, separating the subject from the verb it agrees with: "The birthday cake, as well as 50 birthday candles, is or are arriving soon?" Cake is singular, candles is plural and closer to the verb, so they want "are." They love, love, love separating the subject from the verb it needs to agree with.
- A noun phrase subject with more than one noun in it: "The shape of the statue's ears: is or are unique?" The real subject is "shape"; "ears" at the end pulls them to "are."
- The letter s doing two jobs at once: s for ownership and s for plural at the same time (the cliff's edges). Kids can know both separately, up and down, but once s is there for more than one reason it gets complicated. It's a pressure thing: a lot of students miss these, including high-performing students who say "I can't believe I missed this" and keep missing it, because they think it's easy, don't think that hard, and move on.
- Homophones, especially its and it's: the possessive "its" ends in s, so they really want to write "it" apostrophe s for ownership. Lydia makes this mistake herself and knows she does. Note (Lydia, for the paper): AI does not experience homophones the way humans do; this is a place AI has a hard time empathizing with the human experience.
- Legacy, paper SAT only (Lydia has not seen it on the digital SAT in a while): faulty comparisons, comparing apples to apple trees. "The acrobatic moves are similar to the karate's" instead of "those in karate"; comparing the tree to the apple instead of apple to apple or tree to tree.

## Ratios, rates, proportional relationships, and units

- Ratios are difficult when they aren't out of a whole. Like if we are comparing 3 apples to 4 oranges, depending on the question, the ratio could be 3:7, 4:7 or it could be 3:4. That's confusing.
